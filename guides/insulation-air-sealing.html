Insulation and air sealing: the cheapest energy upgrade

New heat pumps and solar panels get the headlines, but the cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one your home never loses. Air sealing and insulation reduce the heat that escapes in winter and enters in summer, and they make every other upgrade work better.

By the Fresh.Energy editorial teamUpdated 2026-10-307 min readSources: government energy agencies & national labs
  • ENERGY STAR estimates sealing and insulating can save up to 10% on annual energy bills.
  • Seal air leaks first, then add insulation. Insulation does little against moving air.
  • The attic is usually the best place to start, followed by the basement rim joist and ducts.
  • Each extra inch of insulation saves less than the one before, so the first upgrades pay back fastest.

Why air sealing comes first

Most insulation works by trapping still air. When air is moving through it, such as warm indoor air rising through gaps in the ceiling into the attic, the insulation acts like a furnace filter rather than a barrier. That is why you often see darkened, dusty patches of attic insulation above leaky spots.

Warm air rises and escapes through the top of the house, which pulls cold air in through gaps lower down. This "stack effect" means leaks at the top and bottom of the home matter most. Sealing them reduces drafts, improves comfort and helps prevent moisture problems in the attic.

The biggest leaks to find and seal

  • Attic bypasses: gaps around plumbing vents, wiring holes, chimneys, recessed lights, and the tops of interior walls.
  • Attic hatch or pull-down stairs: weatherstrip the edges and add rigid insulation to the top.
  • Rim joist: where the house framing meets the foundation in a basement or crawlspace.
  • Ducts: joints and seams in unheated attics, garages and crawlspaces.
  • Doors, windows and outlets: weatherstripping, caulk and foam outlet gaskets.

Materials

Caulk works for small, stationary gaps under about a quarter inch. Low-expansion spray foam fills larger gaps. Rigid foam board or sheet metal with high-temperature sealant covers big openings and areas around chimneys and flues, which need fire-rated materials and clearances. Duct joints should be sealed with mastic or foil tape, not cloth duct tape.

Insulation basics: R-values

Insulation is rated by R-value, its resistance to heat flow. Higher is better. Common types differ in how much R-value they provide per inch:

Insulation typeApproximate R-value per inchTypical uses
Fiberglass battsAbout R-3 to R-4Open walls, attic floors, DIY-friendly
Blown celluloseAbout R-3.2 to R-3.8Attic floors, dense-pack walls
Blown fiberglassAbout R-2.2 to R-4Attic floors
Rigid foam boardAbout R-4 to R-6.5Basement walls, rim joists, exterior sheathing
Closed-cell spray foamAbout R-6 to R-7Rim joists, tight spaces; also air seals

How much do you need? ENERGY STAR's recommendations for an uninsulated attic range from R-30 in the hottest US climate zone, to R-49 in the warm zones 2 and 3, to R-60 from mixed climates (zone 4) northward. If you already have 3–4 inches, the targets are slightly lower. Canada, the UK and Australia publish their own regional guidance, so check local building recommendations.

Worked example: topping up the attic

Here is a simplified way to see why the first inches matter most. Heat lost through a surface is roughly area × heating degree days × 24 ÷ R-value. Take a 1,000 sq ft attic floor in a climate with 6,000 heating degree days (°F), typical of many northern US cities.

  • At R-11: 1,000 × 6,000 × 24 ÷ 11 = about 13.1 million Btu lost per year
  • At R-30: 1,000 × 6,000 × 24 ÷ 30 = 4.8 million Btu
  • At R-49: 1,000 × 6,000 × 24 ÷ 49 = about 2.9 million Btu

Going from R-11 to R-49 cuts losses by about 10.2 million Btu. With a 95% efficient gas furnace and gas at an illustrative $1.50 per therm (100,000 Btu):

10.2 million Btu ÷ 100,000 = 102 therms ÷ 0.95 = 107 therms × $1.50 = about $161 a year

With a heat pump at a seasonal COP of 2.5 and electricity at $0.17/kWh:

10.2 million Btu ÷ 3,412 = 2,989 kWh ÷ 2.5 = 1,196 kWh × $0.17 = about $203 a year

Now compare an attic that is already at R-30. Going to R-49 saves only 4.8 − 2.9 = 1.9 million Btu, less than a fifth as much. The first upgrade does most of the work.

These numbers are simplified. They ignore framing, attic temperature and air leakage, so real results are usually somewhat lower. Electric resistance, oil and propane heat would show larger savings. Our home energy score tool gives a quick sense of where your home stands.

Quick take: If your attic has less than about R-19 today, topping up is one of the best-value upgrades you can make. If it is already near recommended levels, put your money into air sealing, ducts or the basement rim joist instead.

DIY or hire a contractor?

Weatherstripping, caulking, outlet gaskets and sealing the attic hatch are easy DIY jobs. Sealing attic bypasses is doable for a handy homeowner with good safety habits. Blown-in insulation can be DIY with rented equipment, though many people find a contractor's price competitive once labor and mess are counted.

Hire a professional for:

  • Spray foam, which requires protective equipment and proper ventilation during application.
  • Dense-pack wall insulation in finished walls.
  • Any attic with suspected vermiculite insulation, which may contain asbestos, or old knob-and-tube wiring, which should not be buried in insulation.
  • Homes where you plan aggressive air sealing and have fuel-burning appliances.

Ventilation and combustion safety

As you tighten a home, stale air, moisture and pollutants have fewer ways out. Bathroom and kitchen fans should vent outdoors and be used. Very tight homes may need dedicated mechanical ventilation such as a heat or energy recovery ventilator.

If you have a gas or oil furnace, water heater or fireplace that draws combustion air from inside the house, a professional should test that it still vents safely after major air sealing. Keep working carbon monoxide alarms on every level.

Getting started

Start with a DIY home energy audit to find your worst leaks and measure attic insulation. Seal first, insulate second, and then right-size any new heating or cooling equipment, such as a heat pump, to your improved home. Many utilities and local programs subsidize insulation and air sealing; check our incentives page.

Frequently asked questions

Can a house be too airtight?

A house can be too tight without proper ventilation. The goal is to build tight and ventilate deliberately, using exhaust fans or a heat recovery ventilator rather than relying on random leaks.

Should I replace my windows to save energy?

Window replacement is expensive relative to its energy savings. Air sealing, weatherstripping and insulation usually deliver more savings per dollar. Replace windows when they are failing or for comfort and noise reasons.

Can I put new insulation on top of old insulation?

Usually yes, as long as the old insulation is dry and not vermiculite. Seal air leaks before adding the new layer, and do not use faced batts with a vapor retarder on top of existing insulation.

How do I know how much insulation I have?

Measure the depth in several places in the attic and multiply by the approximate R-value per inch for that material. If the insulation is level with or below the tops of the ceiling joists, you likely need more.
