Heat pump water heaters: are they worth switching to?

A heat pump water heater uses the same principle as a heat pump for your home, pulling warmth from the surrounding air into a tank of water. For homes with a standard electric tank, it is one of the most reliable bill-cutting upgrades available.

By the Fresh.Energy editorial teamUpdated 2026-10-306 min readSources: government energy agencies & national labs
  • The US Department of Energy says heat pump water heaters can be two to three times more efficient than conventional electric resistance models.
  • They need a space that stays between 40°F and 90°F (4–32°C) year-round and around 1,000 cubic feet of surrounding air.
  • Payback is fastest when replacing an electric resistance tank; against gas it depends on local prices.
  • They cool and dehumidify the room they sit in, which can be a bonus or a drawback.

How a heat pump water heater works

A conventional electric water heater pushes current through heating elements, turning one unit of electricity into one unit of heat. A heat pump water heater, sometimes called a hybrid water heater, has a small compressor and fan on top of the tank. The fan draws room air across a cold coil, the refrigerant absorbs that heat, and the compressor concentrates it and transfers it into the water.

Most units also contain backup resistance elements. When you run a lot of hot water quickly, or the air around the tank is too cold, these elements kick in. Most models let you choose between modes such as heat pump only, hybrid, and high demand.

Efficiency ratings

In North America, water heaters are rated by Uniform Energy Factor (UEF). Standard electric tanks score a little below 1. Gas storage tanks typically score well below 1 because some heat goes up the flue. Heat pump models commonly carry UEF ratings several times higher than either. The yellow EnergyGuide label on any US model shows its estimated yearly energy use and cost under a standard usage pattern, which is the easiest way to compare models side by side.

Where a heat pump water heater fits

Location matters more than with any other water heater. According to the US Department of Energy, these units should sit somewhere that stays between 40°F and 90°F all year and has at least 1,000 cubic feet of air around the unit. That is roughly a 12 × 12 foot room with a 7-foot ceiling.

  • Good spots: basements, large utility rooms, garages in mild climates, and rooms near a furnace or other source of waste heat.
  • Tricky spots: small closets (unless the unit is ducted or the door is louvered), unheated garages in cold climates, and spaces next to bedrooms.
  • Clearance: the units are taller than many standard tanks, so measure ceiling height.
  • Drain: like an air conditioner, the unit produces condensate that needs a drain or a condensate pump.
Quick take: If your water heater lives in a roomy basement or utility room, you are probably a strong candidate. If it is in a cramped closet, ask an installer about ducted or split models before ruling it out.

Running costs: a worked example

Suppose your current electric resistance tank uses about 4,000 kWh a year. Check your own model's EnergyGuide label, since usage varies with household size and habits. We will use electricity at $0.17/kWh, close to the 2025 US residential average reported by the US Energy Information Administration, and natural gas at an illustrative $1.50 per therm.

Electric resistance tank: 4,000 kWh × $0.17 = $680 a year

Heat pump water heater: Real-world use is typically well under half the energy of a resistance tank. Assume a conservative 30%, allowing for some backup element use: 4,000 × 0.30 = 1,200 kWh × $0.17 = $204 a year

Savings: $680 − $204 = $476 a year

If the heat pump model costs about $2,000 more installed than a like-for-like electric tank, the simple payback is $2,000 ÷ $476 = about 4.2 years, before any rebates.

What about replacing a gas tank?

The resistance tank above delivers roughly 93% of its 4,000 kWh into the water, or about 3,720 kWh of useful heat. That is 3,720 × 3,412 Btu = about 12.7 million Btu, or 127 therms. A gas tank with a UEF around 0.62 would need 127 ÷ 0.62 = 205 therms × $1.50 = about $307 a year.

Against gas, the savings in this example are about $100 a year, so payback is much longer. The case for switching from gas usually rests on other factors: your gas tank is failing anyway, you plan to remove gas service entirely, you have rooftop solar, or local rebates close the price gap. Installation considerations

Electrical supply

Most full-size heat pump water heaters run on a 240-volt circuit, the same as a typical electric tank, so a straight swap is often simple. Newer 120-volt plug-in models are designed for homes replacing a gas tank where there is no 240-volt circuit nearby. They heat more slowly, so a larger tank may be recommended. Any new circuit or panel work should be done by a licensed electrician.

Sizing and recovery

Heat pump mode heats water more slowly than resistance elements or gas burners. Many installers suggest going one size up in tank capacity, for example from 50 to 65 gallons, to avoid relying on backup elements during busy mornings. A mixing valve lets the tank store hotter water safely, which effectively increases capacity.

Noise and cooling

The fan and compressor make a hum comparable to a dehumidifier or small window air conditioner. The unit also cools the room it sits in. In a hot climate or a damp basement that is welcome. In a cold climate where the tank shares space with heated living areas, your heating system will make up some of that heat, which slightly trims the net savings.

Is it worth switching?

For most households with an electric resistance tank, yes, and the best time to switch is before the old tank fails. Emergency replacements often push people toward whatever is in stock. Plan ahead: check the space, measure clearances, and line up a plumber who has installed these units before.

If you heat water with gas, the decision is closer. It often makes sense to combine this upgrade with others, such as a heat pump for space heating or induction cooking, so you can drop the gas connection and its fixed monthly fee. Pairing with solar panels can also improve the economics.

Rebates for heat pump water heaters vary widely by location, and some US federal tax credits ended after 2025. Frequently asked questions

Can a heat pump water heater go in a garage?

In mild climates, often yes. In places where the garage regularly drops below about 40°F, the unit will rely on its backup elements and lose most of its efficiency advantage.

Do heat pump water heaters run out of hot water faster?

They recover more slowly in heat pump mode. Choosing a slightly larger tank or using hybrid mode during high-demand periods usually solves this.

Are they noisy?

They produce a steady hum similar to a dehumidifier. In a basement or utility room it is rarely noticeable; next to a bedroom it may be.

Do they need extra maintenance?

Beyond normal tank care such as periodic flushing, you will need to clean the air filter and make sure the condensate drain stays clear.
