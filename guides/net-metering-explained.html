Net metering, net billing and export rates explained

Your solar panels will often make more power than your home is using at that moment. What your utility pays for that surplus, whether full retail value, a fraction of it or almost nothing, can change your savings by a third or more.

By the Fresh.Energy editorial teamUpdated 2026-10-307 min readSources: government energy agencies & national labs
  • Net metering credits exports at or near the full retail rate, usually netted over a month or year.
  • Net billing and export tariffs credit exports at a separate, usually lower, rate.
  • California's 2023 move from net metering to net billing cut typical export values by roughly three-quarters, according to most analyses.
  • The lower your export rate, the more it pays to use your own solar on site, through timing, smart controls or a battery.

The two flows that matter

Every solar home has two separate streams of energy:

  • Self-consumed solar: power your home uses as it is generated. It never reaches the meter, so it saves you your full retail rate, including any per-kWh delivery charges.
  • Exported solar: surplus power that flows to the grid. Its value depends entirely on your utility's compensation rules.

The share of output you self-consume varies widely with system size, battery storage and when people are home. A household out all day with a large system exports more; a home office with daytime cooling exports less.

Netting periods

Under net metering, timing matters. With monthly netting, surplus in one month offsets imports in the same month and any leftover rolls forward. With annual netting, summer surplus can cover winter bills, with a "true-up" at the end of the year. Excess beyond your annual use is often paid at a much lower rate or forfeited, which is why oversizing rarely pays.

Net billing in practice

Under net billing, imports and exports are tracked separately. You pay the full rate for every kWh you import and earn the export rate for every kWh you send out. When California's three large investor-owned utilities moved new solar customers to net billing in April 2023, export credits were set from an avoided-cost model with hundreds of different hourly values across the year. Exports in late afternoon and evening in summer can be worth much more than exports at midday.

Worked example: one system, three sets of rules

A home uses 9,000 kWh a year and pays 20 cents per kWh. Its solar produces 10,000 kWh a year. It uses 55% of that directly and exports the rest.

  • Self-consumed: 10,000 × 55% = 5,500 kWh, saving 5,500 × $0.20 = $1,100
  • Exported: 4,500 kWh
  • Still imported from the grid: 9,000 − 5,500 = 3,500 kWh
Rule setValue of 4,500 kWh exportedTotal annual value
Annual net metering (retail credit for exports up to the 3,500 kWh imported; 1,000 kWh annual excess paid at 4¢)3,500 × $0.20 + 1,000 × $0.04 = $740$1,840
Net billing at an average 7¢ export rate4,500 × $0.07 = $315$1,415
Low export tariff at 3¢4,500 × $0.03 = $135$1,235

Even generous net metering does not pay full retail for annual excess, so this system is slightly oversized for net metering. Under net billing, the same system is worth about 23% less, and the export side drops by more than half. Plug your own rates into our solar savings calculator to see the effect.

Quick take: Under net metering, size solar to roughly match your annual use. Under net billing or low export tariffs, size it closer to your daytime use and focus on shifting consumption into sunny hours.

Charges solar cannot offset

Check your tariff for charges that apply no matter how much solar you produce:

  • Fixed monthly customer or connection charges.
  • Minimum bills, which set a floor on what you pay.
  • Non-bypassable charges, per-kWh fees on all imports that some utilities exclude from export credits.
  • Grid access or capacity charges specific to solar customers in some areas.

These reduce the share of your bill solar can touch and are worth confirming before you size a system.

How to get more value under low export rates

  1. Shift flexible loads to midday. Run the dishwasher, laundry, pool pump and water heater when the sun is up.
  2. Charge an EV from solar if the car is home during the day. See our EV home charging guide.
  3. Pre-cool or pre-heat with a heat pump in the afternoon to reduce evening imports.
  4. Consider a battery if the gap between your import and export rates is large. Our home battery guide shows the arithmetic.
  5. Choose the right rate plan. Many solar customers are required or encouraged to take time-of-use rates, which can reward afternoon exports and penalize evening imports.

Grandfathering and future changes

Export rules change. Many utilities let existing customers keep their original terms for a set number of years after a rule change, often called grandfathering or legacy treatment. The length varies. When you evaluate solar, assume rules may become less generous during the system's life, and check whether your interconnection date affects the rules you get.

Finding your local rules

Your utility's website and rate schedules are the definitive source. Search for "net metering," "net billing," "distributed generation" or "export" along with your utility's name. In Great Britain, the Energy Saving Trust publishes impartial guidance on the Smart Export Guarantee. For US federal, state and utility incentives that can affect payback, see our incentives page.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between net metering and net billing?

Net metering credits exported power at or near the retail rate you pay for imports. Net billing credits exports at a separate, usually lower, rate while you pay full price for everything you import.

Do I get paid for extra solar power at the end of the year?

Sometimes. Many net metering programs pay annual excess at a low wholesale or avoided-cost rate, and some forfeit it. Sizing your system close to your annual use avoids wasting surplus.

Is solar still worth it under net billing?

It can be, but the case depends more on self-consumption. Right-sizing the system, shifting loads into sunny hours and, where rate gaps are large, adding a battery can restore much of the value.

Can my utility change my net metering terms after I install solar?

Rules can change, but many regulators protect existing customers for a period. Check your utility's legacy or grandfathering provisions before you sign a contract.
